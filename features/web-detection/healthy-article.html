A long weekend in the lakes

Weekend notes, filed in the spring edition

The overnight train from the coast leaves a little after nine and reaches the mountains just as the sun clears the ridge. Sleeper cabins hold four bunks, and the attendant brings tea and a paper bag of biscuits before the lights go down. If you can, book a lower bunk on the left side of the carriage: the morning view across the lake is worth setting an alarm for, even on a short night.

Most travellers rush through the old market town on their way to the hiking trails, which is a shame. The covered hall still hosts a cheese and honey market every Thursday, the church tower is open to climb for a small donation, and the bakery beside the bridge sells a walnut loaf that keeps for days in a rucksack. Give it an afternoon, and stay for supper at one of the inns on the square.

Packing light for a week of walking comes down to a few rules. Choose clothes that dry overnight, carry one warm layer rather than two thin ones, and leave the second pair of shoes at home unless the route crosses rivers. A small first aid kit, a paper map of the area, and a refillable bottle cover most surprises. Everything else can usually be bought in the next village along the way.

A good stock is the quiet foundation of winter cooking. Roast the bones and vegetables until they are deeply browned, then cover them with cold water and bring the pot up slowly. Skim the surface during the first half hour and never let it boil hard, or the liquid will turn cloudy and greasy. Four hours later, strain it through a fine sieve, cool it quickly, and freeze it in portions for soups and sauces.

Bread dough tells you when it is ready if you know what to look for. After the first rise it should have roughly doubled, feel airy under the hand, and spring back slowly when pressed with a floured finger. If the dent fills in at once, give it more time; if it does not recover at all, it has risen too far, and the loaf will spread flat in the oven. Shape it gently and let the second rise be shorter.

Lentils are the most forgiving pulse in the cupboard. Unlike beans they need no soaking, and the small green and brown kinds hold their shape after half an hour of gentle simmering. Cook them with a bay leaf and a halved onion, season only at the end, and dress them while still warm with olive oil, lemon, and plenty of chopped parsley. They keep for several days in the fridge and improve with time.

Before a long ride, spend five minutes on the bike rather than an hour fixing it at the roadside. Check the tyre pressure, squeeze both brake levers hard, spin each wheel to see that it runs true, and look along the chain for stiff links. A drop of oil on the chain and a wipe with a rag afterwards will keep the drivetrain quiet, and the gears will shift cleanly for weeks.

Climbing is mostly about rhythm. Shift into an easier gear before the slope bites, stay seated for as long as you can, and breathe steadily rather than in gasps. Standing on the pedals feels faster but burns energy quickly, so save it for short, steep ramps. On a long climb, pick a landmark a few hundred metres ahead, ride to it, then pick the next one. The summit arrives sooner than you expect.

A dripping tap is almost always a worn washer or cartridge, and replacing it takes less time than finding the right part. Turn off the water at the isolation valve under the sink, open the tap to drain the pipe, and remove the handle. Take the old washer to the hardware shop so you can match its size exactly. Reassemble everything in reverse order and tighten by hand before a final quarter turn.

Painting a room goes faster when most of the effort is spent before the first coat. Fill the cracks, sand the patches smooth, wash the walls with sugar soap, and tape the edges carefully. Cut in around the ceiling and skirting with a brush, then roll the open areas in overlapping vertical strokes while the edges are still wet. Two thin coats always look better than one thick one.

Late summer is the best time to see the Milky Way from the northern hemisphere. Get away from street lights, give your eyes twenty minutes to adapt to the dark, and look south after midnight. The bright band runs from the horizon up through the Summer Triangle, three bright stars that are easy to pick out even from a town. A pair of ordinary binoculars reveals star clusters scattered along its length.

Meteor showers need no equipment at all, only patience and a comfortable chair. Lie back, look at a broad patch of sky about halfway up, and avoid staring at the radiant itself, since the longest trails appear some distance away from it. Expect quiet spells followed by bursts of several meteors in a minute. Dress warmly even in August; a clear night loses its heat quickly.
